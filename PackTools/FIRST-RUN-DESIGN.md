# First-Run Design — The First Five Minutes

Status: design only, no code. Sits above PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md (the in-client chat panel) and MCP-DESIGN.md (the tools it calls). Those assume a player who already knows what a "pack" is and wants one. This doc is about the player who doesn't — the one who just downloaded an installer from a website and has never seen this before. If the first five minutes don't produce delight, the rest of the stack (schema, compiler, sandbox, sharing) never gets used.

## 0. What this is not

Not a redesign of the chat panel, not a new tool, not a change to the schema or compiler. This is the sequence of screens/moments between "installer finishes" and "player has made and placed something they're proud of." If this doc starts redesigning `edit_tree_node` or the pack format, that's scope creep — MCP-DESIGN.md and SCHEMA.md own that.

## 1. Prior art — what real onboarding did, named

### Two different problems, both relevant, kept separate on purpose
This project has both at once: **(a)** classic game onboarding — a new player learning a world and its systems, the problem The Sims/Minecraft/Roblox/Dreams/Animal Crossing all solved — and **(b)** AI-product onboarding — a user given a capability they've never had before ("describe a thing, get a thing"), which behaves nothing like a game tutorial and has its own recent, well-measured failure mode. Treating this as only a game-onboarding problem misses (b); treating it as only an AI-onboarding problem misses that the player is also new to a whole simulation game, not just a text box.

### Game onboarding: creative/sandbox precedent

**The Sims' own opening (CAS → house → drop into life)**: never a blank lot with a blank prompt. The player builds a person first (bounded, guided choices — body type, then face, then clothes, one axis at a time), gets dropped into a *pre-furnished* starter house, and the "make something" impulse doesn't arrive until the game has already proven it's fun to just live in. **Adopt**: sequence identity/context before creation-with-consequences; don't open on the hardest, most open-ended tool first.

**Minecraft**: default world-join has *no* tutorial at all — survival mode drops you in cold. What actually teaches new players is almost entirely external (wikis, YouTube) and Minecraft's own built-in "Tutorial World" (a separate, opt-in, scripted sequence with signposted rooms: here's crafting, here's a chest, here's combat) that most players skip because the base game is legible enough without it. **Adopt**: nothing directly — this is the "sink or swim, letting the community absorb the teaching cost" model, and this project doesn't have Minecraft's decade of ambient cultural literacy to lean on; a brand-new mechanic (natural-language object creation) has no wiki yet by definition. **Ignore**: shipping with no guided path and hoping players figure it out.

**Roblox Studio**: opens on a template gallery (baseplate, obby, tycoon, etc.), not a blank scene — new creators pick a *starting point* they can already recognize the shape of, then modify. This is the same instinct as Dreams below, independently arrived at. **Adopt**: templates/starting points over blank canvas, generalized in §3 below to "a guided first request" since this project's medium is language, not a 3D scene — there's no literal template gallery to browse, but the equivalent move is offering a short list of concrete example prompts instead of an empty box.

**Dreams (Media Molecule)**: the most directly relevant precedent, because Dreams is *also* "give non-programmers a real creation tool disguised as a game." New creators are never handed a blank canvas — they start from a template, follow a narrated, video-overlay walkthrough of one tool at a time, and are explicitly told (paraphrased from the studio's own guidance): start simple, don't try to do too much too fast. Only after that on-rails first pass do they take what they made and put their own stamp on it. **Adopt directly**: (1) narrated walkthrough rather than a wall of text — matches PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §2's "narrate progress in-character, never as a build log" instinct, extended to the *first* creation specifically; (2) start from a template/first project, personalize second, not "blank prompt first."

**Animal Crossing**: not a creation tool at all in the early game, but the best-in-class example of *pacing capability reveals over time* — Tom Nook hands the player one task at a time across the first several in-game days (pitch a tent, pay off a bell debt in small chunks, meet neighbors) and the "you can now design your own patterns/build bridges/terraform" capabilities unlock gradually, each with its own small, low-stakes first use. **Adopt**: capability reveal doesn't have to be a single onboarding screen — "you can make things" can be introduced as a discoverable moment (see §3) rather than a forced first-launch modal, and later capabilities (remix/sharing, per SHARING-DESIGN.md) can unlock even later once the player trusts the first one.

### Spectatorship before participation

**Minecraft's build-watching creator economy (Grian and similar)**: a large chunk of Minecraft's audience consumes creation purely as *spectatorship* — watching someone else build, with no competition, no stakes, nothing for the viewer to do but watch something appear. Grian's channel is reported around ~8.9M subscribers and ~2.8B views on exactly this format (reported stats, not independently verified here — treat as directional, not a cited figure to repeat as fact). The pattern that matters regardless of the exact numbers: people will watch creation at scale *before* attempting it themselves, and watching is often the thing that makes them want to try. None of the precedents above (Sims, Roblox Studio, Dreams, Figma) put a passive watch-only beat before the user's own first attempt — they all go straight to the user doing something, guided or not. **Adopt, tentatively**: a first-run demo the player *watches* rather than immediately being asked to prompt — see §3's revision below. **Caveat**: Grian's audience chose to watch, repeatedly, for entertainment — a forced watch-first beat inside a game's onboarding is a different, more captive context, and risks reading as friction/a loading screen if it's not skippable. Treat the adoption as "make it available and default-on," not "make it mandatory."

### AI-product onboarding: the actually-new problem

This project's core mechanic — describe something in language, get a real artifact — has no precedent in the game-onboarding examples above, because none of them involve generation with real cost, real latency, and real failure risk on the very first interaction. Recent, measured data on exactly this:

- **The "blank prompt problem"**: generative AI tools that open on an empty prompt field and expect the user to write their own request from scratch see a measured **68% day-one churn rate** ([Sibanu Bora, Medium/Bootcamp](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/the-blank-prompt-problem-why-ai-products-are-failing-their-first-session-5e74bcb00b09)). A blank text box reads as a test the user can fail, not an invitation.
- **Guided/templated first input measurably outperforms blank input**: products using predefined templates or starter prompts at onboarding saw a **45% increase in task completion rate** versus an open text field ([userpilot.com](https://userpilot.com/blog/best-user-onboarding-experience/)).
- **Figma's onboarding asks what you want to create first**, then narrows/adjusts the workspace toward that answer rather than dropping the user into the full general tool — a "guided choice, then bespoke first experience" pattern, not a menu of fixed templates ([userpilot.com](https://userpilot.com/blog/best-user-onboarding-experience/)).
- **Notion treats the empty state itself as the onboarding surface** — a blank page isn't actually blank, it carries a warm, human-voiced prompt plus visible shortcuts/template suggestions inline, so the user is never staring at true emptiness even when the canvas looks empty ([userpilot.com](https://userpilot.com/blog/best-user-onboarding-experience/)).

**Adopt**: never a true blank prompt on first use — offer 3-4 concrete example requests as tappable starting points, Figma-style ("what do you want to make?" with recognizable options, not a fill-in-the-blank test). **Ignore**: fully autonomous "six out of ten AI tools skip guides and show the prompt field immediately" as a default for *this* product specifically — that pattern works when the request is cheap and instant (a chatbot reply); this project's request costs real money and takes real seconds (see §4), which is exactly the combination the blank-prompt-problem data says is highest-risk for churn.

## 2. The throughline

Every creative-tool precedent (Dreams, Roblox Studio, Figma) independently converged on the same move: **the first creative act is guided, not blank — a template, a menu, or a narrowed question, never an empty box.** The AI-onboarding data explains *why*, with numbers: an empty box in front of a first-time user isn't neutral, it's actively worse than a constrained choice (68% churn vs. 45% better completion). For this project specifically, two extra constraints (real cost, real latency — §4) push even harder toward guided-first than any of the precedents above needed to push, because a blank prompt here isn't just a UX risk, it's a *cost* risk the moment a first-time player asks for something absurd ("make me a working car with weapons") that burns budget without producing something placeable.

The spectatorship finding above adds a step *before* this throughline rather than replacing it: guided-not-blank is still the right shape for the first prompt whenever the player does provide one, but Minecraft's build-watching audience suggests the very first move might not be a prompt at all — it might be watching one happen. That doesn't change §1's "never a blank box" conclusion; it adds an optional beat in front of it (§3).

## 3. Recommended flow

### Landing: lot first, not creation first
Per §1's Sims-opening precedent — land the player in a normal, already-alive lot (their existing save, or a starter lot on first-ever launch), not a special "welcome to creation mode" screen. The whimsy PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §1 is built on ("a player already in FreeSO, looking at a blank spot on the floor, is the moment the idea happens") only works if the game is already running normally when the idea strikes. Don't gate normal play behind an onboarding flow for a feature the player hasn't asked for yet.

### First discovery: surfaced, not forced
Per the Animal Crossing pattern (§1) — don't open on a mandatory first-launch modal explaining the creation system before the player has done anything else. Surface it contextually: a "Make Something" catalog entry/button (already scoped as UI work in PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §1) that's visible but not interrupting, discovered the way a player discovers any other Buy Mode feature. The first time it's opened *is* the first-run moment this doc designs for — not app launch.

### First open of "Make Something": watch one happen, then make one
Per §1's spectatorship finding, revise the flow to add a beat before the player is ever asked to type anything: the *first* time the panel opens, play a short, scripted demo — narration streams, an object appears, exactly the real end-to-end experience, just triggered by the system instead of the player's own request. This does two things the guided-example-chips step alone doesn't: (1) it's a stronger pull into trying it than a menu of options is, per the Grian pattern of "watching creates the desire to attempt"; (2) it teaches normal latency *implicitly* — the player sees generation take a few seconds once, before it's ever their own request on the line, which is a better teacher than the explicit "this takes a sec" string this doc originally proposed (see below, §3 "during generation" is revised accordingly). **Must be skippable, one tap, no penalty** — per the caveat in §1, a captive audience is not a Grian audience, and forcing the watch defeats a design built around whimsy and player agency (PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §1). Flagging the skip-vs-mandatory framing as the actual product decision in §6, since "make it skippable" is a recommendation, not a settled default players might route around anyway (e.g. does skipping cost anything, or is it truly free to bypass).

### First prompt: guided choice, never a blank box
Directly applying §1's AI-onboarding data (68% churn on blank vs. 45% better completion on guided) and Roblox Studio/Figma's template-first pattern: the chat panel's first-ever open shows **3-4 concrete, tappable example requests** instead of (or alongside) an empty text field — e.g. "a garden gnome that gossips with you," "a plant that needs watering," "a lamp that changes color when you're sad." These aren't arbitrary — pick ones that exercise a genuinely charming, cheap, reliably-successful path through the existing pack primitives (a `clone_from_guid` appearance + one or two interactions, nothing that stresses `test_in_vm`'s failure modes). The player can still type their own request instead of tapping one — the examples are a floor, not a cage — but the *default* first-open state is never a true empty box.

**We control the first request more than that**: recommend going one step further than Figma's "ask what you want, then narrow" — for the literal first-ever creation, offer the example chips *and* let the player freely type, but treat whichever they choose as "the guided first," meaning the agent takes the most conservative, most reliably-successful path through pack authoring for that one request (fewer interactions, safer generator params) even if a more elaborate build was implied. Save "make it exactly as elaborate as they described" for request two onward, once the player has seen one success and trusts the system. This directly mirrors Dreams' "start simple, don't try to do too much too fast" guidance (§1) — but applied to the *system's* choices, not the player's, since the player never sees pack internals to simplify themselves.

### During generation: narrate, and say what's normal
PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §2 already specifies in-character narration over a build log ("giving him something to say...," never "calling edit_tree_node"). Revised given the watch-first beat above: if the player watched the scripted demo, they've already felt real generation latency once before it was ever their own request on the line — that experience is a better teacher than any string of copy, so the explicit "this takes a little while" framing this doc originally proposed for *every* first-timer is now only needed as a fallback for players who skip the demo. For skippers specifically, keep a single, low-key expectation-setter before narration starts ("this takes a little while, feel free to keep playing") — still a one-time, first-run-only affordance, never a persistent loading-bar UI. Players who watched the demo can go straight to narration-only, per PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md's steady-state design, since they no longer have "no prior" — they have one data point, which is enough.

### On failure: never a dead end, never silent
PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §5 already leans toward "silent retry, narrate only the outcome" for `test_in_vm` failures generally — reasonable for players who've already succeeded once and trust the system. For the *first-ever* attempt specifically, that's not enough on its own, because a first-time player has no accumulated trust to draw on if the very first thing they see is "hm, that didn't work, trying again" with no visible progress. Recommend: if the first attempt needs more than one self-correction pass (per MCP-DESIGN.md §2/§3's validate→fix loop), the narration should still stay in-character but acknowledge effort, not just silence ("still working on getting him to talk right...") rather than going quiet — the player shouldn't be able to tell the difference between "slow" and "stuck" on attempt one. And if a first attempt exhausts retries and genuinely fails (should be rare, since §3 already routes first-timers down the most reliable path): don't return an empty-handed dead end — offer one of the guided examples as a fallback ("that one's tricky — want to try the gnome instead, or tell me more about what you pictured?"), so the first-run floor is "the player got *something* delightful," even if not literally what they first asked for.

### After success: object appears, placement is the payoff
Per PLAYER-LAYER-DESIGN.md §2 step 5 — object lands in inventory/catalog, placeable immediately. No extra "congratulations" screen or achievement modal needed; the payoff *is* placing the gnome and watching it work, which is more Sims-native than a UI celebration would be. Consider (not required for MVP) a single, brief "try talking to it" nudge right after first placement — closes the loop from "I described a thing" to "I used the thing," which is the actual point being sold in the first five minutes.

## 4. The two constraints that shape this, and how

**Cost (~$0.30+/object today, per Kat, being optimized)**: an unlimited blank prompt on first run isn't just weaker UX per §1's data, it's a real spend-risk the moment a first-timer's request is deliberately elaborate or adversarial before the player has any sense of what's reasonable to ask for. §3's "guided-first-request-takes-the-conservative-path" recommendation is partly a cost control, not just a UX one — the system, not the player, is deciding how elaborate the *first* build gets. Whether to cap or throttle requests-per-new-account beyond that first-request conservatism (a rate limit, a free-tier count before requiring... something) is a real product/business decision this doc isn't making — flagged in §6 for Kat, since it's pricing/business-model territory, not onboarding-flow territory.

**Latency (real seconds, narration helps but a first-timer doesn't know that's normal)**: addressed in §3, now two-tiered — the watch-first demo teaches normal latency implicitly for players who see it (they experience the wait once with nothing at stake before it's their own request), and the explicit "this takes a little while" framing from the original design remains as a fallback for players who skip the demo. Either path is cheap to build (a scripted demo sequence, or one first-run-only string) and both target the same risk named in the brief: a first-time player with no prior for "how long should this take" reading multi-second latency as a broken feature rather than a working one.

## 5. What's borrowed vs. new

**Borrowed directly**: Sims' own CAS→house→life sequencing, generalized to "land in a normal, already-alive world before any creation moment" (§3 landing); Dreams' narrated-walkthrough-over-text and "start simple" first-project philosophy (§3 first prompt, during-generation); Roblox Studio's and Figma's guided-choice-over-blank-canvas pattern (§3 first prompt); Animal Crossing's paced-capability-reveal instead of a mandatory tutorial modal (§3 first discovery); the AI-onboarding literature's specific, measured guidance against blank prompts (§1, §3 first prompt, directly citing the 68%/45% figures rather than intuiting the direction); Minecraft build-watching content's "spectatorship precedes participation" pattern, adapted into the watch-first demo beat (§3 "watch one happen, then make one").

**New here, not borrowed from anywhere**: none of the precedents in §1 combine *all three* of "non-programmer creator," "real per-request dollar cost," and "real multi-second generation latency" in one first interaction — Dreams' tools are instant and free per-use, Figma/Notion's AI features are typically flat-rate-subscription-covered rather than metered per creative act the way this is, and no game-onboarding precedent has a cost dimension at all. The specific combination in §3 — system-chosen conservative first request (cost control) + one-time explicit latency framing (expectation control) + guided-example-fallback-on-failure (trust control) — is assembled for this project's specific constraint stack, not lifted wholesale from any single precedent.

## 6. Open questions for Kat

- **Tone of the example prompts and the narration strings themselves** — "a garden gnome that gossips with you" is a placeholder guess at the right register (whimsical, Sims-native, not cute-generic-AI-assistant-voice); the actual copy is a taste call, not something to finalize here.
- **Rate limiting / free-tier shape beyond the first-request conservatism (§4)** — is there a cap on requests per new account before some gate kicks in (payment, waitlist, cooldown), or is early cost absorbed as an acquisition expense? Real business-model decision, not guessed at here.
- **Does the "try talking to it" nudge after first placement (§3) exist at all, or is even that too much hand-holding for the tone Kat wants?** Recommended as optional/light-touch specifically because it's the one place in this doc where "helpful" risks tipping into "hovering" — her call on where that line sits.
- **Whether returning players (not first-ever-launch, but first-launch-after-an-update that adds a new capability, e.g. sharing) get any of this guided treatment again, or whether guided-first is strictly a one-time, one-feature thing.** Animal Crossing's pattern (§1) suggests later capabilities deserve their own small reveal moment, not a repeat of this exact flow, but that's a call for whoever designs the sharing-feature rollout (SHARING-DESIGN.md's lane), flagged here only because it's adjacent.
- **Is the watch-first demo (§3) skippable-but-default-on, or does it need to be even lighter — e.g. only shown if the player hesitates at the blank-ish panel rather than always playing first?** This doc recommends default-on-and-skippable as the safer read of the Grian evidence (§1's caveat: a captive audience isn't the same as an audience that chose to watch), but the exact trigger condition and whether "skip" needs to be one tap or zero taps (auto-advance if the player starts typing mid-demo) is a taste call on how much this product trusts its own first ten seconds — Kat's call, not guessed here.
- **The Grian/spectatorship figures (~8.9M subs, ~2.8B views) are reported, not independently verified in this research pass** — the *pattern* (watching precedes attempting) is worth designing around regardless, but if this becomes a pitch-deck or investor-facing claim rather than an internal design rationale, the numbers need their own verification pass first.
